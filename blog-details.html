Five clauses worth reading twice before you sign

Most commercial disputes trace back to a handful of provisions that nobody argued about at the time. Here is what each one does when things go wrong.

28 July 2026 · 7 min read · Advantage Law Firm

Nobody negotiates every line of a commercial agreement. Time and goodwill both run out, and most of a contract is machinery that never gets used. But a small number of provisions decide what happens when the relationship stops working — and those are almost never the ones people spend their negotiating capital on.

If you only have an hour with a draft, spend it on these five.

1. Limitation of liability

This is the clause that determines what a breach is actually worth. It usually does three things at once: caps total liability at a figure, excludes whole categories of loss, and sometimes carves out particular obligations from both.

Read it against the worst realistic outcome, not the likely one. The questions to ask:

  • Is the cap a fixed sum, or tied to fees paid? Fees paid in the first months of a contract can be very small.
  • Does the exclusion of “indirect” or “consequential” loss sweep up something you would consider direct?
  • Is the cap mutual, or does it only protect one side?

A cap you could live with is fine. A cap you have not read is how a substantial claim turns out to be worth a fraction of the loss.

2. Termination

Two distinct things get bundled here: termination for cause, and termination for convenience. They behave very differently.

For cause, look at whether a breach must be “material”, whether there is a cure period, and how long it runs. A thirty-day cure period sounds reasonable until you are the one waiting thirty days while the problem continues.

For convenience, the question is simply who has it and on what notice. An arrangement where one side can walk on thirty days' notice and the other is committed for three years is a very different deal from the one most people think they signed.

More claims are lost on notice provisions than on the merits. The right to do something usually depends on having said so, in the right way, in time.

3. Notice provisions

The least interesting clause in any agreement and the one that most often decides matters. It sets out how formal communications must be given: to what address, by what method, and when they count as received.

The failure pattern is consistent. A party raises a problem by e-mail to their usual contact, believes they have given notice, and discovers months later that the contract required a letter to a registered office. By then a deadline has passed.

Two things are worth doing at signature:

  1. Make sure the permitted methods include how you will realistically communicate.
  2. Note the address and the deemed-receipt timing somewhere you will find it later.

4. Variation and change control

Most agreements say they can only be varied in writing and signed by both parties. Most working relationships then vary them constantly by e-mail, in meetings, and by simply doing things differently.

That gap is where a large share of commercial disputes live. One side points to the contract; the other points to eighteen months of accepted practice. Both have a point, and resolving it is expensive.

Where a contract governs work that will genuinely change — construction, development, long-term supply — a change control procedure that people will actually follow is worth more than a strict clause they will ignore.

5. Governing law and dispute resolution

Usually the last clause in the document and usually agreed in about ninety seconds. It determines which law applies, where any dispute is heard, and whether it goes to court or to arbitration.

The practical consequences are large: cost, timescale, whether proceedings are public, and how easily a decision can be enforced against assets. If the other party is in another jurisdiction, this clause matters far more than its position in the document suggests.

A practical order of work

When a draft lands and time is short, this is a sensible sequence:

  1. Read the liability cap and exclusions. Establish the maximum exposure.
  2. Read termination. Establish how you get out and how they do.
  3. Read notices. Note the method, address and timing.
  4. Read the payment and delivery obligations that actually bind you.
  5. Read the dispute clause. Confirm it is somewhere you can live with.

Everything else can wait for a second pass. These five decide what the agreement is worth on the day it stops going well.
